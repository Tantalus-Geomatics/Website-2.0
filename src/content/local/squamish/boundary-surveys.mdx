**What our survey provides.** For a boundary survey in Squamish, the survey usually covers:

- **The record.** The title, the plan that created the lot, and the plans and field notes behind it.
- **The lines.** The lot lines the BC Land Surveyor adopts from the posts and the other evidence on the ground. When you need it, the plan also shows the distance from a fence, a building or a driveway to those lines.
- **Marks you can see.** A post at a corner that needs one, and stakes for a fence or a layout. A wooden stake is not the legal post.
- **A plan when a corner is posted.** When a new post replaces a missing corner, we prepare a posting plan and file it at the Land Title Office.

**How a boundary survey works in Squamish.** Marking an existing lot is not a subdivision. The District's Approving Officer is not the person who decides where an existing line is. The order below is the survey itself.

1. **Research.** The title, the plan that created the lot, and the charges that run along a line, such as a statutory right of way, so a fence is not put across one.
2. **The search.** On the valley floor, original posts are often buried under later fill. On the benches, in places such as Garibaldi Highlands and Valleycliffe, a post may be in rock, bush or a hedge. How long the search takes depends on the age of the plan and on the ground.
3. **The decision.** The BC Land Surveyor weighs the posts that are found, the marks that are gone, and occupation such as an old fence. The bearings and distances on the plan are part of that evidence. A fence does not, by itself, move the line.
4. **Marking.** Corners that need a legal post get one. Corners you need to see while a fence is built get a stake as well.
5. **The plan.** When a new post replaces a missing corner, we prepare a posting plan that records the posts found and the posts set, a BC Land Surveyor signs it, and we file it at the Land Title Office.

**What people ask us to mark.** A fence on the line. A building that has to sit off the line. A hedge or a driveway that may already cross it. The [Zoning Bylaw No. 2200, 2011](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/68132/) is where the setback distance is written. The survey shows where the line is. We do not give you the setback number. The District reads that number when you apply for a [building permit](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/building-permit/).

**Who the plan is for.** You, your builder, and your lawyer or notary. The District sees the line when a permit application needs it. A survey that only marks an existing lot does not create a new lot. Creating lots is a [subdivision survey](/squamish/services/subdivisions-surveys/).

**The ground.** Squamish sits where the Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers meet the head of Howe Sound. Fill on the valley floor is the usual reason a post is hard to find. Higher up, at Garibaldi Highlands, Valleycliffe and along the creek ravines, the search is in rock and bush. Land on the [Cheekye River debris-flow fan near Brackendale](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/cheekye-fan/) has its own history of deposition. We treat that ground as part of the search, not as a reason to skip it.

**Older plans.** Re-establishing older boundaries in Squamish often involves two issues. On the valley floor, original survey posts are often buried under later fill, so finding them takes careful searching. Older plans also may not match what is on the ground today, such as fences, buildings or driveways. We research the plan history and reconcile it with the evidence we find before any line is marked.
