**What our survey provides.** For a Squamish subdivision, the survey usually covers:

- **Site Survey Plan:** Showing existing buildings, and other relevant site features, and their distances to existing lot lines and charge boundaries (Right of ways, covenants, easements). This includes tying the survey to a provincial geodetic datum, so that ground and floor elevations can be read on the same datum as the District's FCL mapping. Additionally, this often includes locating top of bank, watercourses and natural boundaries where the District or a Qualified Environmental Professional asks for them.
- **Proposed Subdivision Plan:** showing existing and proposed buildings, and their distances to existing and proposed lot lines on the site plan. This is a necessary component of the subdivision application process.
- **Final Subdivision Plan:** preparing the subdivision plan for the Approving Officer's review and for deposit at the Land Title Office.

**How subdivision works in Squamish.** Inside the District of Squamish, a Council-appointed Approving Officer reviews a subdivision. The steps below are the usual municipal order. Each municipality sets its own submission requirements, so we check the current versions at the start of each project, and we check which development permit areas apply to the site. Outside the District, in an electoral area, a provincial Approving Officer with the Ministry of Transportation and Transit approves the subdivision, and we check the applicable electoral-area OCP for its development permit areas.

1. **Review with planners.** Before an application is filed, the applicant and the BC Land Surveyor review the proposal with the District's planners. On a municipal subdivision, that review covers zoning, minimum lot size and dimensions, trees, steep slopes and geotechnical considerations, proximity to watercourses, lot servicing, access, and park dedication.
2. **Preliminary application.** A preliminary application to subdivide is submitted to the District. It includes a topographic survey of the lot or lots (the site survey plan above), a proposed subdivision plan, a current title search, and copies of any charges, liens, or notices on title. The proposed subdivision plan shows the dimensions of the subject lot and the dimensions and areas of the proposed lots. The topographic survey and the proposed subdivision plan can often be combined. A development permit, a development variance permit, or rezoning may also be required.
3. **Approving Officer review.** The Approving Officer, planning, and other departments review the application. If the Approving Officer gives tentative approval, a letter sets out the conditions of the subdivision. Conditions may include charges and charge plans, such as restrictive covenants, easements, and rights of way, road closure plans, and road or park dedication plans.
4. **Field survey and plan preparation.** The BC Land Surveyor retraces the subject lot and completes the site survey, then surveys the subdivision, any roads or parks, and any charges, unless a charge is dealt with by an explanatory plan. The surveyor prepares the subdivision plan and the charge plans. Draft copies go to the Approving Officer, the planner, or the client or legal representative for review and feedback.
5. **Certification.** Once the plans are approved, the BC Land Surveyor prepares a digitally signed Survey Plan Certification form and an affixed survey plan image for each plan to be submitted.
6. **Approving Officer's signature.** The certified plans are submitted to the Approving Officer, either directly or through the applicant or legal representative, along with the related documents. The Approving Officer signs the plans where a signature is required.
7. **Deposit at the Land Title Office.** After the Approving Officer approves the plan, the client's legal representative (their lawyer or notary) registers the plan at the Land Title Office (LTSA).

**Who approves it.** Inside the District of Squamish boundary, subdivision applications are reviewed by an Approving Officer appointed by Council. The District's [subdivision page](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/land-development-applications/subdivision/) suggests starting with a conceptual site plan, and application steps are set out in its [Land Development Procedures Bylaw No. 2632](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/). Land outside the boundary is in an electoral area of the Squamish-Lillooet Regional District. There, [subdivisions are approved by a provincial Approving Officer with the Ministry of Transportation and Transit](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/governments/local-governments/planning-land-use/land-use-regulation/subdividing-land), and the [SLRD reviews referrals](https://www.slrd.bc.ca/planning-building/planning-development-services/development-applications-approvals/subdivisions-slrd). We confirm early which authority applies, because it changes the application path. Where the land is in an electoral area, we check the applicable electoral-area OCP for its development permit areas.

**Which bylaws apply.** The District's requirements include the [Subdivision and Development Control Bylaw No. 2649, 2018](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/), the [Zoning Bylaw No. 2200, 2011](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/68132/), the [Squamish2040 Official Community Plan (Bylaw No. 2500, 2017)](https://squamish.ca/projects-plans-and-initiatives/master-plans/ocp/), and the [Floodplain Management Bylaw No. 3184](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/). The OCP designates the District's development permit areas. Examples named in the OCP include Development Permit Area 1, Environmental Protection, and Development Permit Area 11, Protection from Wildfire Hazard. Form-and-character development permit areas also exist where the OCP maps them. The District's [development permits page](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/land-development-applications/development-permits/) covers development permit applications. We don't summarise the bylaws or the development permit area guidelines here, because they are amended from time to time. We check the current versions at the start of each project, and we check which development permit areas apply to the site.

**The ground.** Squamish sits where the Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers meet the head of Howe Sound. Parts of the valley floor are covered by the District's flood hazard mapping, which follows its [Integrated Flood Hazard Management Plan](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/integrated-flood-hazard-management-plan/), adopted in 2017. Flood Construction Levels can be looked up on the District's [map viewer](https://gis.squamish.ca/map/index.html). The OCP designates Development Permit Area 2, Protection from Flood Hazard, for this flood hazard (Part 5; mapped on Schedule L-1), alongside Floodplain Management Bylaw No. 3184. Neighbourhoods higher on the valley sides, such as Garibaldi Highlands and Valleycliffe, have steeper ground, exposed rock and creek ravines. Land on the [Cheekye River debris-flow fan near Brackendale](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/cheekeye-fan/) is mapped by the District as a debris-flow hazard area.

**Older plans.** Re-establishing older boundaries in Squamish often involves two issues. On the valley floor, original survey posts are often buried under later fill, so finding them takes careful searching. Older plans also may not match what is on the ground today, such as fences, buildings or driveways. We research the plan history and reconcile it with the evidence we find before any new lot lines are laid out.
