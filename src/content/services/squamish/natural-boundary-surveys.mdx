**What our survey provides.** For a natural boundary survey in Squamish, the survey usually covers:

- **The present natural boundary.** The line where the ordinary action of the water has marked the bank, in the soil and in the vegetation. We locate it on Howe Sound and on the Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers, and on creeks that cross a lot.
- **The line on title.** Where the registered plan shows the natural boundary, and how that line sits against the present bank.
- **What has changed.** Ground added or lost slowly, a channel that moved at once, or fill and walls placed on the foreshore. We show which of those we can see. Who holds the ground is set out below. We do not decide it on site.
- **A plan the next step can use.** The present bank on a site plan, so a setback can be measured from it. When the titled line has to be redrawn on a plan deposited at the Land Title Office, that plan is a subdivision plan or a reference plan. The application goes to the Surveyor General before the plan is submitted. The [Land Title Practice Manual, section 94](https://ltpm.ltsa.ca/94-endorsement-certificate) describes that endorsement.

**How a natural boundary survey works in Squamish.**

1. **Research.** We read the plan that made the water a boundary, and the Crown grant where a copy can be had, and we check whether the water was inside the parcel. Older air photos, when we can get them, show an earlier bank.
2. **The bank.** We walk the present bank and locate the natural boundary. On a tidal shore that line is not the water's edge on the day of the survey. On a river it is not the edge of the water after a storm.
3. **Fill, dikes and walls.** Fill or a wall on the foreshore is drawn as its own line. It is not drawn as a new natural boundary. A dike and a Flood Construction Level can both matter to a building under the [Floodplain Management Bylaw No. 3184](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/). Neither one is the property line at the water.
4. **The plan.** For a [building permit](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/building-permit/) or a [development permit](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/land-development-applications/development-permits/), the present natural boundary goes on the site plan. The setback distance is set by the District, and often by a Qualified Environmental Professional, not by the survey. If the boundary on the title has to be redrawn, we prepare a subdivision plan or a reference plan and apply to the Surveyor General for an endorsement. The land title office is not where that application starts. After the Surveyor General signs, the plan is submitted to the land title office. The practice manual points to Circular Letter No. 477A, 6 April 2020, for the material that application asks for.

**Who uses the line.** The District, when a water setback is part of a permit. A Qualified Environmental Professional, when a streamside setback is measured from the top of bank or from the natural boundary. Your lawyer or notary, when the title still shows an old bank. Development Permit Area 1, Environmental Protection, in the [Squamish2040 Official Community Plan](https://squamish.ca/projects-plans-and-initiatives/master-plans/ocp/) is the area that usually raises the watercourse question. Development Permit Area 2 raises the flood question. Those are different lines from the natural boundary. A site plan that carries the bank for a building application is described on the [topographic surveys](/squamish/services/topographic-surveys-and-site-plans/) page.

**The ground.** The Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers meet Howe Sound at Squamish. The Mamquam, including the reunion spawning channel, the Cheakamus, the Stawamus, and the creeks in the ravines above Valleycliffe and the Highlands, each have a bank that moves. The survey records the bank we find on the day of the field work. Dikes and training works along the rivers are mapped when they stand on or beside the parcel. They are not a substitute for the natural boundary. The District's flood mapping is published on its [map viewer](https://gis.squamish.ca/map/index.html).

**Fill is not a new line.** Placing fill, rock or a retaining wall out from the bank does not move the natural boundary out to the new edge. An excavation, such as a boat basin cut through the bank, does not move it either. The plan shows the wall or the cut, and the natural boundary, as separate lines. A line left by fill, a cut, a flood or a landslide is not, by itself, the natural boundary.

**Accretion, erosion and a sudden change.** These words describe how the bank moves. They are not a decision we make in the field about who owns the ground. The description follows the Land Title Practice Manual and the Association of British Columbia Land Surveyors' 2026 natural-boundary course. It is not a legal opinion on a particular title.

- **Accretion.** Land added slowly, so slowly that you cannot watch it happen from hour to hour, growing out from the existing bank by the ordinary action of the water. The water may drop back, or soil may settle against the bank. That added land can belong to the upland parcel even when the title still shows the older bank. It is not accretion if a channel separates the new ground from the bank, if the ground was placed as fill to make new land, or if it arrived all at once.
- **Erosion.** Land lost slowly, in that same gradual way, from the existing bank, by natural processes rather than by a machine. As that happens, the lost ground goes back to whoever owns the bed. That owner is often the Crown. It is not always the Crown.
- **A sudden change.** A sudden loss or gain, including a channel that moves at once, does not move the natural boundary. The boundary stays where it was before the event. Years later the new edge can look like a natural bank, so the survey looks for the older line.

**Who holds the ground between the lines.** If the change was slow accretion, the added land can be part of the upland. If it was slow erosion, it can return to the owner of the bed. If it was sudden, or if it was fill or a wall, the boundary does not move to the new edge. When a plan of the new line is deposited at the Land Title Office, the Surveyor General may endorse it, if that is in the public interest. For accreted land next to Crown land, the endorsement is that the land has lawfully accreted. For a water boundary that differs from the plan the title is based on, the endorsement is that the line is the natural boundary, where the Surveyor General is satisfied the new line is the correct one and the older plan was short on detail or wrong for another good reason. The registrar does not open a separate title for accreted land. It is brought into the title for the adjoining upland. On an accretion plan the old bank is still shown. On a natural-boundary adjustment the plan shows the present bank. There is a separate rule for some subdivision plans and reference plans that leave a remainder partly under water, next to provincial Crown land: that covered part of the remainder can return to the Crown when the plan is deposited. We locate the lines. We do not decide the title.
