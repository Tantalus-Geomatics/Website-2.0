**What our survey provides.** A UAV survey in Squamish measures the site from the air and brings that measurement onto the same drawing as the ground survey. It usually covers:

- **LiDAR and photographs.** Dennis Sherman, BCLS, P.Eng., of Tantalus Geomatics Land Surveying Ltd., flies a DJI M400 with LiDAR and imaging sensors. The LiDAR is the source for ground elevations under and between the trees. The photographs become an orthophoto and, on open ground, a surface.
- **Ground control.** Marks measured by GNSS or a total station, so the flight sits on the same datum as the lot lines and the District's mapping.
- **A signed plan, when the job needs one.** Contours and an orthophoto, and a plan signed by a BC Land Surveyor when the flight is part of a site plan or another survey.

**How a UAV survey works in Squamish.**

1. **Whether the aircraft can fly the site.** Before we fly, we check the site. Near Squamish Airport and elsewhere, that check uses tools such as the NRC Drone Site Selection Tool, NAV CANADA's NAV Drone web tools, the Canada Flight Supplement and the Canada Water Aerodrome Supplement, among other resources. We do not fly a job the current rules do not allow.
2. **The flight plan.** The area, the height and the overlap. A flight on the steep ground above Valleycliffe is not the same flight as a flight on the valley floor.
3. **Marks on the ground.** We set and measure control, and we tie the work to the lot when the product has to show legal lines. The aircraft does not find an iron post under fill. That search is done on foot. It is the same search described on the [boundary surveys](/squamish/services/boundary-surveys/) page.
4. **The flight.** We fly the DJI M400, which carries LiDAR and imaging sensors.
5. **Processing.** We classify the LiDAR so the ground can be separated from the canopy, build the surface and the orthophoto, and compare them with the ground control. Then we draft what the project needs.

**Where a flight helps.** Large lots, steep banks, creek ravines, and bush where walking the ground is the slow part. A small lot on an open street is often still a ground survey. LiDAR helps where the laser can reach the ground between the trees. It does not replace a boundary survey.

**The ground.** Squamish sits where the Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers meet the head of Howe Sound. The valley floor is diked and, in places, filled. The benches, including Garibaldi Highlands and Valleycliffe, are steeper, with rock and timber. Those two kinds of ground ask for different flights. The valley walls, the timber and Squamish Airport are all on the flight plan for a site. The details come from the airspace check for that parcel. Land on the [Cheekye River debris-flow fan near Brackendale](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/cheekye-fan/) is mapped by the District as a debris-flow hazard area, and a surface of that ground is one of the things a flight can measure.

**What the flight is not.** It is not a property line, and it is not a flood construction level. The District's FCL mapping and [Floodplain Management Bylaw No. 3184](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/) decide the flood question. The flight supplies ground elevations those numbers can be compared with, on a datum named on the drawing. When the flight is part of a site plan, that drawing is described on the [topographic surveys](/squamish/services/topographic-surveys-and-site-plans/) page.
