**What our survey provides.** For a topographic survey or site plan in Squamish, the survey usually covers:

- **Existing conditions.** Buildings, driveways, retaining walls, fences, the visible surface utilities, and the trees a designer has asked us to locate, with spot elevations and contours.
- **Lot lines and charges.** Distances from those features to the lot lines, and to rights of way, covenants and easements on title, after the lines have been retraced.
- **Elevations on a named datum.** Ground and floor elevations on a geodetic datum written on the plan, so they can be compared with the District's Flood Construction Level mapping. \[CONFIRM\] The District's published FCL layer is labelled CGVD28. A 2021 consolidation of Subdivision and Development Control Bylaw No. 2649 asked design-drawing elevations to be on CGVD2013. We read the current bylaw, and we ask which datum this drawing should use, before field work starts.
- **Water and banks.** Top of bank, watercourses and the present natural boundary, where the District or a Qualified Environmental Professional asks for them.

**How a topographic survey works in Squamish.** The plan is a drawing of the ground for a building permit, a development permit, or a design. It is not filed at the Land Title Office. The District's submission list changes, so we read the current [building permit](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/building-permit/) and [development permit](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/land-development-applications/development-permits/) pages at the start of each job.

1. **Research.** The title, the registered plan, and the charges that limit where a building can sit. We also look up the parcel on the District's [flood and hazard map](https://gis.squamish.ca/map/index.html).
2. **What the drawing needs.** We agree the features, the contour interval and the datum with you and, where there is one, with your designer. A building-permit drawing often has to show the building, the setbacks, grades at the building and the floor elevation. \[CONFIRM\] The District's "Minimum Requirements for Building" PDF lists those items, and it says the building official may waive the survey plan. We check the current PDF. We do not decide a waiver.
3. **Field work.** We search for the posts that define the lot, then measure the features with GNSS, a total station, or both. On a large or heavily treed site we may add a drone flight. That work is on the [UAV mapping](/squamish/services/uav-mapping/) page.
4. **The plan.** We draft the site plan or plan of topography, name the datums, and a BC Land Surveyor signs it for the application.

**Who the plan is for.** Your designer uses it to draw the project. The District uses it to read the application against zoning, flood construction levels and development permit areas. A survey that posts a missing property corner is a different job, described on the [boundary surveys](/squamish/services/boundary-surveys/) page.

**Which bylaws apply.** The drawing is read against the [Zoning Bylaw No. 2200, 2011](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/68132/). Where the land is in the mapped floodplain, the [Floodplain Management Bylaw No. 3184](https://squamish.civicweb.net/filepro/documents/19302/) and Development Permit Area 2 in the [Squamish2040 Official Community Plan](https://squamish.ca/projects-plans-and-initiatives/master-plans/ocp/) are part of the review. Development Permit Area 1, Environmental Protection, is the area that usually puts a watercourse or a top of bank on the plan. We do not summarise the bylaws. We check the current versions, and which development permit areas cover the site.

**The ground.** Squamish sits where the Squamish, Mamquam, Cheakamus and Stawamus rivers meet the head of Howe Sound. On the valley floor the survey is often about fill and flood construction levels: the plan has to show ground and floor elevations on a datum that can be set beside the District's FCL mapping. The District publishes those levels on its [map viewer](https://gis.squamish.ca/map/index.html), following its [Integrated Flood Hazard Management Plan](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/integrated-flood-hazard-management-plan/). Neighbourhoods higher on the valley sides, such as Garibaldi Highlands and Valleycliffe, are steeper, with rock, creek ravines and long hedges. Land on the [Cheekye River debris-flow fan near Brackendale](https://squamish.ca/building-and-land-development/home-land-and-property-development/cheekye-fan/) is mapped by the District as a debris-flow hazard area.

**Hedges, walls and fill.** A Squamish site plan often spends as much time on a cedar hedge, a retaining wall or buried grade as on the house. On the valley floor, later fill can bury both the original ground and the survey posts, so the search for posts is part of this survey.
