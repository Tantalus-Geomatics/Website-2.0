British Columbia still measures heights on CGVD28, the Canadian Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1928. GeoBC anticipates adopting new vertical and horizontal datums in 2030. Tantalus Geomatics does all of its survey work in CGVD28 unless a client explicitly requests another datum. We will adopt CGVD2013 only once it is the official vertical datum in use in British Columbia.

This page is for landowners, designers and engineers who have to read an elevation on a plan, or set one beside a flood construction level. It follows the three public sources linked at the end.

## What is changing

A vertical datum is the starting surface for a height. CGVD28 was built by spirit levelling, working inland from tide gauges. The Province's [height-transformation page](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/data/geographic-data-services/geo-spatial-referencing/height-transformations), updated 10 March 2026, still names CGVD28 as British Columbia's current vertical datum.

None of this moves a house, a benchmark or a river bank. It changes the numbers used to write down where something is, and how high it is.

## Why the change is being made

The Province's [information paper](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/data/geographic/georeferencing/vertical_modernization_info_paper.pdf) on Canadian height modernization sets out the limits of CGVD28. The benchmark network is costly to maintain, and federal levelling has been minimal since 2001. The network does not reach remote parts of the province. The paper reports regional distortions of up to one metre on the primary network, and larger differences, a few metres to several metres, on local networks in remote parts of British Columbia. CGVD28 also fits poorly with satellite positioning. A GNSS receiver measures a height above an ellipsoid. A geoid model is what turns that into an elevation.

CGVD2013 was built so heights could come from that geoid, and so they would agree with GNSS. The Province's current page says the HTv2.0 model is what to use when a GNSS height has to be brought onto approximate CGVD28 elevations.

## When the change is anticipated

The [Surveyor General's report](https://ltsa.ca/surveyor-generals-report-abcls-agm-2026/) to the Association of British Columbia Land Surveyors in March 2026 says the fieldwork to improve physical control monuments is finished, and that GeoBC anticipates adopting the new vertical and horizontal datums in 2030.

The older provincial information paper expected a federal geoid datum around 2009, and provincial adoption over a long transition it wrote as 2009 to 2020/30. The date used here for Squamish work is the later one: GeoBC's anticipated adoption in 2030.

## What it means for an elevation on a drawing

An elevation without a datum name is not a complete number. The same point has a different height on CGVD28 than on CGVD2013. The information paper expected the new primary-network heights to differ from published CGVD28 heights by less than one metre at any one point in Canada, and by more than 10 centimetres at most places. On secondary networks in British Columbia it allowed for larger differences, a few metres to several metres in remote areas. As an illustration, with heights near Toronto held fixed, it expected about 55 centimetres higher in Vancouver, about 5 centimetres lower in Montreal, and about 30 centimetres lower in Halifax. Those figures are that paper's estimate of the size of the change. They are not the shift on a particular lot in Squamish. The shift for a site is a transformation.

The height of a floor above the ground beside it was expected to stay at the precision of the survey that measured it. What changes is the number written when that height has to be stated against the national datum.

## Flood construction levels

A flood construction level is an elevation. The information paper lists floodplain work among the uses of a height system. A drawing can be set beside a flood construction level only when both elevations name the same datum. The District of Squamish flood construction level maps are CGVD28. These sources do not say that the District has republished those maps on CGVD2013, and this page does not say that a District-wide CGVD2013 requirement is in force. Where a client or the District asks for works-and-services drawings under the [Subdivision and Development Control Bylaw](https://squamish.civicweb.net/document/200228), those drawings use CGVD2013. That is a works-and-services requirement. It is not the datum of the flood maps.

## What we do now

Tantalus Geomatics does all of its survey work in CGVD28 unless a client explicitly requests another datum. We will adopt CGVD2013 only once it is the official vertical datum in use in British Columbia. A change to that official vertical datum is anticipated in 2030. If a designer or an engineer needs the same points on another datum, we can provide that transformation on request.

Public tools already cover part of this. The Province publishes HTv2.0 for approximate CGVD28 elevations, including a Metro Vancouver hybrid model.

How elevations are shown on a Squamish site plan is on the [topographic surveys](/squamish/services/topographic-surveys-and-site-plans/) page.
